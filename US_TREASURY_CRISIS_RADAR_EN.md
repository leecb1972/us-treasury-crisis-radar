# Do not predict "when Treasuries will collapse."

::: sub
Monitor when the United States moves from **being able to issue large
amounts of debt** to **having to pay increasingly higher prices to issue
new debt**.

::: notice
**Core principle:** \$40T+ of debt is not, by itself, a crisis signal.
The radar watches whether **financing price + term premium + fiscal cash
flow + growth + marginal demand** deteriorate together.

## The "crisis triangle"

::: triangle
**Term Premium ↑** + **g − r ↓** + **Interest / Revenue ↑**

If all three deteriorate together, the problem is shifting from "there
is a lot of debt" toward "the marginal price of new debt is rising."

## Ferguson Limit --- strategic-fiscal indicator

Historian Niall Ferguson proposed "Ferguson's Law": when a great power
spends more on debt servicing than on defense, it risks ceasing to be a
great power. The **Ferguson Limit** is the point at which interest
payments exceed defense spending.

This is useful as a **national-power / fiscal-capacity signal**, but it
is not the same thing as a Treasury default-risk threshold. It should
not be interpreted as a mechanical prediction that decline has already
begun.

## Do not predict "when Treasuries will collapse": monitor the price of financing

### Abstract

Discussion of U.S. Treasury debt often falls into two extremes: one
expects a sudden collapse after debt reaches some number; the other
argues that the United States can borrow indefinitely because it issues
the world's dominant reserve currency. A more useful question is not
"when will Treasuries collapse?" but when the United States moves from
being able to issue large amounts of debt to having to pay increasingly
higher prices to issue new debt.

### 1. \$40T+ is not a crisis by itself

Debt stock is a stock variable. Stress appears through financing
conditions. It is also incorrect to multiply all outstanding debt by
today's 10-year yield because Treasuries have different maturities,
coupons and refinancing schedules.

### 2. The constraint is real resources, not simply dollars

The ability to issue dollars is a major U.S. advantage, but the
government cannot create oil, chips, electricity, housing, engineers or
productivity by decree. Persistent fiscal expansion can eventually show
up through inflation, long-term yields, term premia or forms of
financial repression.

### 3. Why the term premium matters

A higher 10-year yield is not automatically a fiscal crisis. It may
reflect stronger growth, inflation or expectations for future short
rates. A more concerning pattern is weak growth or monetary easing while
long yields remain elevated and term premia rise.

### 4. g − r: can the economy outrun debt costs?

A simplified debt dynamic is
`Δ(Debt/GDP) ≈ Primary Deficit + (r − g) × Debt/GDP`. If nominal growth
stays below the effective financing cost while primary deficits persist,
stabilizing debt becomes harder.

### 5. Interest / Revenue: how much future fiscal space is already committed?

Net interest as a share of federal revenue is intuitive: for each dollar
of federal revenue, how much is being absorbed by the cost of past
borrowing?

### 6. Are foreign investors really leaving?

A change in one country's Treasury holdings is not the same as a global
selloff. Separate official reserves from private capital and Treasury
holdings from the broader stock of dollar assets. Watch persistent
changes in marginal long-duration demand.

### 7. Ferguson Limit: a different question

Niall Ferguson\'s "Ferguson's Law" adds a strategic-fiscal lens. His
formulation says that when debt-servicing costs exceed defense spending,
a great power risks ceasing to be a great power. He calls that crossing
point the **Ferguson Limit**. The name refers to 18th-century Scottish
philosopher Adam Ferguson, not Niall Ferguson himself.

The Ferguson Limit should therefore be treated as a **strategic capacity
warning**, not as a technical default trigger. It asks whether debt
service is beginning to compete with a core component of national power.
The Treasury Crisis Radar asks a different question: whether the
marginal price of new borrowing is rising enough to constrain fiscal and
monetary choices.

### 8. Five signals deserve high attention

**10Y yield ↑ + Term Premium ↑ + Interest/Revenue ↑ + g−r ↓ + foreign
marginal demand ↓** is more informative than any single indicator.

### 9. Five-stage framework

**Stage 1:** large borrowing remains inexpensive; **Stage 2:** borrowing
remains available but becomes more expensive; **Stage 3:** interest
costs increasingly crowd out fiscal space; **Stage 4:** fiscal-dominance
risk rises; **Stage 5:** adjustment may occur through fiscal
consolidation, financial repression, inflation, higher nominal growth,
or some combination.

### Conclusion

> The signal worth waiting for is not a "collapse date." It is a
> persistent rise in the marginal price of debt, reinforced by worsening
> fiscal capacity.

## Methodology

1.  **Debt stock:** how large is the debt?
2.  **Fiscal flow:** are deficits still creating new debt?
3.  **Financing price:** are 10Y/30Y yields persistently rising?
4.  **Risk compensation:** is the term premium rising?
5.  **Growth differential:** is `g−r` turning negative?
6.  **Fiscal capacity:** is Interest/Revenue rising?
7.  **Marginal demand:** are foreign/private investors reducing
    long-duration Treasury demand?
8.  **Strategic capacity:** has the Ferguson Limit been crossed, and is
    the gap widening?

`Illustrative Score = 20% Term Premium + 20% g−r + 20% Interest/Revenue + 20% Fiscal Deficit + 20% Market Demand`

The score is a research framework, not an official risk measure or
investment signal. Ferguson Limit is kept outside the composite because
it measures strategic-fiscal capacity rather than marginal Treasury
pricing.
